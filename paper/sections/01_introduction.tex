\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

% Questions 1--2: problem, significance, and difficulty.
Cooperative multiagent reinforcement learning (MARL) offers a way to train
robot teams for tasks that require coordinated physical interaction, such as
transporting an object that one robot cannot move alone. Learning this
coordination is difficult when agents receive a shared reward after a sequence
of joint actions: the outcome obscures which decisions helped the team and
which depended on teammates' behavior~\cite{foerster2018coma}. In tightly
coupled tasks, a useful individual decision can receive little feedback until
other agents discover complementary behavior~\cite{rahmattalabi2016dpp}.
Cooperative box pushing illustrates this difficulty. Reaching a box is
insufficient; agents must establish compatible contact and act together to
move it. An agent approaching a useful pushing position may therefore receive
the same unsuccessful outcome as one pursuing an unrelated task. Effective
credit assignment must distinguish an unproductive decision from one whose
value has yet to be realized through cooperation.

% Questions 3--4: prior work and the specific remaining problem.
Hierarchical reinforcement learning separates goal selection from primitive
control on different timescales, as in FeUdal
Networks~\cite{vezhnevets2017fun}. Feudal Multi-Agent Hierarchies apply this
manager--worker organization to teams~\cite{ahilan2019fmh}.
Counterfactual methods address attribution from another direction: COMA
compares one agent's action with alternatives while holding teammates' actions
fixed~\cite{foerster2018coma}, whereas D++ evaluates hypothetical additional
agents to reward behavior that could become useful with
support~\cite{rahmattalabi2016dpp}. Goal interactions and effects over time
are also addressed by CM3's multi-goal credit function~\cite{yang2020cm3} and
MACD's imagined counterfactual trajectories~\cite{chai2024macd}. For Feudal
coordination, the remaining challenge is crediting a manager-generated goal
for the future return it could produce under alternative teammate commitments.
In dynamic manipulation, this requires considering the behavior induced by
joint goals, including the time needed to provide support and the assignments
teammates would abandon.

% Questions 5--6: proposed method and its central insight.
We introduce a D++-inspired method for assigning credit to goals in Feudal
MARL. A manager assigns each worker a bounded spatial waypoint for a fixed
horizon. Workers learn from intrinsic rewards for reducing their distance to
the waypoint, while the manager learns from team rewards; both levels use
proximal policy optimization~\cite{schulman2017ppo}. A centralized model
learns to predict team advantage from the joint state and waypoint assignments.
For each focal agent, we retain its assigned goal and construct counterfactual
joint assignments that redirect its nearest teammates toward its current
position, leaving the remaining goals unchanged. The largest positive
predicted gain per redirected teammate augments the focal agent's manager
advantage. This formulation asks whether a goal would be more valuable with
support, using goals as compact descriptions of behavior over time. Because
support comes from reassigning existing teammates, the comparison asks the
model to account for travel and forgone assignments. The resulting term
deliberately shapes the manager's objective toward goals with predicted
cooperative potential. Counterfactual assignments are evaluated through model
queries during training; workers execute the manager's original waypoints.

% Questions 7--8: empirical findings and contributions.
We plan to evaluate this mechanism in continuous-control cooperative box
pushing, comparing it with flat multiagent PPO (MAPPO)~\cite{yu2022mappo},
a Feudal hierarchy using shared team credit, and a counterfactual baseline
that replaces only the focal worker's goal. Dense and sparse rewards, uncoupled
controls, and larger coalitions will test whether the signal for cooperative
potential improves coordinated task completion.

The methodological contributions are a formulation of counterfactual teammate
support through temporally extended waypoint assignments and a learned
credit signal that incorporates their predicted benefit into the manager's
policy update. The planned empirical analysis will examine when these
counterfactual predictions identify useful cooperation and whether they
translate into improved team performance.
